\begin{abstract}
  \myabstract{
  Monitoring machine learning systems for performance-degrading distribution shifts 
  is crucial. However, getting ground-truth labels for deployment data to evaluate 
  or re-train the model is costly. We propose a way for selecting the most informative 
  instances for manual annotation from a pool of unlabeled instances 
  under a limited labelling budget. Each instance is reduced to a single scalar 
  score using both a normal ResNet-50 classifier and a Hyperplane-Assisted Softmax
  (HASeparator) variant of the same architecture: angular margin to the 
  closest decision boundary for the latter, and softmax confidence for the former. 
  We utilize a $k$-sigma left-tail cutoff, which is the same in form for both models, 
  to identify informative instances. We model the training-set distribution of each 
  score as a Gaussian, defined by its empirical mean and standard deviation. We 
  verify this process in two complementary contexts. We demonstrate that the HAS 
  angular-margin score on CIFAR-10N correlates with human-annotator disagreement 
  far more strongly than the baseline's confidence score, validating it as a 
  trustworthy, label-free signal of which cases are worth relabeling. We demonstrate 
  that prioritizing a fixed annotation budget by the HAS margin recovers 
  target-domain accuracy more effectively than confidence-based or random selection 
  on a real-world domain-shift task (Landscape Recognition $\to$ ARXPHOTO314), with 
  the benefit being most noticeable under the tightest budgets. When combined, these 
  findings suggest the angular margin as a useful signal for selecting which 
  deployment instances to label next. 
}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

For a \term{machine learning (ML)} system deployed in a non-stationary
environment, continuous monitoring and adaptation is a challenging
necessity in order to maintain its performance. Although drift has
been recognized early as a factor for poor
performance \cite{schlimmer-granger:1986}, it was only relatively
recently \cite{quinonero-etal:2008} that the relevant sub-field was
organized into a framework that clearly identifies and names the two
types of drift:
Assuming the most abstract formulation of an
ML model as the conditional distribution $P(Y|X)$ that represents the
relationship between inputs $X$ and outputs $Y$, degradation is either due to
\term{covariate shift} (i.e., change in the distribution of the input values)
or \term{concept drift} (i.e., change in the relationship between inputs and outputs)
or both.

Adjusting to data drift is well-studied task, with a rich literature
on both how to detect the drift and on how to minimize the amount of
labelling necessary in order to re-train or fine-tune the system.


\cite{hira-et::2025}



\cite{bhargava-singhal:2024}



In order maximally economize on labelling effort, it makes sense to be
able to extract as much information as possible from the
\emph{unlabelled} deployment data, and use this information to
pin-point the instances that would have the impact on model accuracy.


Tran et al. \cite{tran-etal:2026}: concept drift either synthetically
simulated or simulated by assuming a more fine-grained labelling (such
as CIFAR-100 or ImageNet) as deployment data after training on CIFAR-10.
The latter simulates novel classes, but not drift in the class boundaries.
Synthetic drift sounds a not-very-good idea, will elabortate.

Noting how CIFAR and ImageNet label refer to individual physical
objects, it is unlikely that a noticeable drift can occur.

We suggest a method for selecting the most informative instances for human 
annotation within a limited labeling budget from a pool of unlabeled instances 
gathered after deployment.
An identical $k$-sigma left-tail cutoff is then applied to both the model under 
evaluation and its Hyperplane-Assisted Softmax counterpart, reducing both to a 
single per-instance scalar score whose training-set distribution is modeled as a 
Gaussian. This ensures that any variation in the outcome reflects the 
informativeness of the underlying score rather than a difference in the detection 
mechanism itself.
We demonstrate that choosing a small fraction of new examples for labeling based 
on the HAS angular-margin score provides accuracy recovery that exceeds 
confidence-based or random selection under the same labelling budget, and that 
this score has a good correlation with true annotation mistake. After deployment, 
we anticipate that this methodology will be a 
useful part of systems for giving human annotation effort priority in ML systems.

\section{Training Setup}
\label{sec:training}

This section provides the optimiser and schedule hyperparameters for the two 
model architectures outlined in Section~\ref{sec:models}.
Both tracks below train the same architectures from scratch on their respective 
source datasets (Section~\ref{sec:datasets}). Per-instance scoring and selection 
procedures are only applied to frozen models after training.


\textbf{Landscape Recognition/ARXPHOTO314 Track.} The models are trained using 
SGD (momentum $\gamma = 0.9$, weight decay $\lambda = 10^{-4}$) and a multi-step 
learning-rate schedule that decays by a factor of 10 at 60\% and 80\% of training 
($T = 150$ epochs). The baseline employs an initial learning rate of $\eta_0 = 0.1$, 
whereas the HAS model utilizes a lower rate of $\eta_0 = 0.05$ to prevent early 
geometric collapse due to the angular penalty. The parameters are $\alpha = 1.0$, 
$\beta = 0.5$, margin $m = 0.3$, and scale $\sigma = 8.0$. Both use a batch size 
of 64. Training augmentation includes random cropping to $224\times224$, horizontal 
flipping, and color jitter ($\pm0.2$ brightness/contrast). Evaluation 
and feature extraction utilize a deterministic resize-and-normalize transform.

\textbf{CIFAR-10N Track.} Both models are trained using SGD (momentum $0.9$, 
Nesterov, weight decay $10^{-4}$) with the identical multi-step schedule 
(decay by 10 at 60\% and 80\% of training), with $T = 100$ epochs and batch size 128. 
The baseline uses $\eta_0 = 0.1$, whereas the HAS model employs $\eta_0 = 0.05$, 
angular margin $m = 1.0$, and scale $\sigma = 3.0$ (the configuration published 
for ResNet-50 on CIFAR-10 in the original HASeparator article), with a 5-epoch 
linear warm-up of $\beta$ (Equation~\ref{eq:warmup}).

After training, the models are frozen and employed in inference to compute 
Section~\ref{sec:scoring} scores for each instance in the training set and 
evaluation pool. In the HAS model, the embedding $\hat{e}_i \in \mathcal{S}^{63}$ 
represents the $\ell_2$-normalized bottleneck output. Baseline embeddings do not 
have this requirement and are only utilized to compute $q_i$, not the geometric 
score.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:outro}

We presented a methodology for identifying, among a pool of unlabelled
deployment instances, those most informative for manual annotation under
a constrained labelling budget.The baseline and HAS models are simplified 
to a single scalar score, modelled as a Gaussian over the training set, 
and thresholded by a same $k$-sigma rule. 
On CIFAR-10N, the HAS angular margin correlates with human-annotator 
disagreement more strongly than baseline confidence (H1). 
In a real-world domain-shift task, ranking annotation budget by HAS margin 
improves target-domain accuracy more effectively than 
similar selection from the Baseline's same unlabeled pool, especially with 
limited budgets (H2).
