\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

For a \term{machine learning (ML)} system deployed in a dynamic
environment, continuous monitoring and adaptation is a challenging
necessity in order to maintain its performance. Since, in its most
abstract formulation, an ML model is the conditional distribution
$P(Y|X)$ that represents the relationship between inputs $X$ and
outputs $Y$, a failure to maintain the performance observed during
development-time testing can mean that either the distribution of the
input features has changed or that the relationship between inputs and
outputs has changed. In this work, we will follow most, although not
all, of the relevant literature and call the former \emph{data drift}
and the latter \emph{concept drift}.





Concept drift, first introduced by Schlimmer and Granger (1986), refers to changes in the
conditional distribution P (y|x), where the relationship between input features x and target
labels y evolves over time in non-stationary environments. In the other hand, data drift (also
known as covariate shift) denotes changes in the input features distribution P (x) (Acker-
man et al. 2020).

\paragraph{Contributions}

We present a methodology for characterizing unlabelled instances as
exhibiting data drift or concept drift (or both) and, in the case of
concept drift, for identifying both the direction and the severity of
the drift. Based on this information, we also present a methodology
for selecting a small subset of the unlabelled new instances for
manual annotation, such that fine-tuning on these sub-set only
achieves accuracy on a par with labelling all new instances and
fine-tuning on the complete new dataset. We envisage that this
methodology will become an important part of systems for monitroing ML
systems post-deployment.
