
To validate our geometric prioritization approach, we utilize two distinct datasets that serve complementary roles: CIFAR-10N for error-sensitivity validation and ARXPHOTO314 for real-world model adaptation.

\subsubsection{Error-Sensitivity Validation: CIFAR-10N}
\label{sec:cifar10n}
We utilize the CIFAR-10N dataset to validate \textbf{H1}. CIFAR-10N provides human-annotated noise labels for the CIFAR-10 training set, capturing the collective consensus of multiple annotators. Unlike standard datasets, this allows us to quantify the \quoted{severity} of an error \textit{i.e., how many humans disagreed with the label}. We use this dataset to demonstrate that our geometric margin $\Delta_i$ acts as a robust proxy for human-verified label noise. By showing that low-margin samples are disproportionately likely to be human-annotated errors, we confirm that the HAS model functions as a reliable error detector without needing explicit ground-truth labels.

\subsubsection{Model Adaptation Task: Landscape \& ARXPHOTO314}
\label{sec:target_datasets}
To demonstrate model adaptation, we evaluate our pipeline on the \textit{Landscape Recognition} dataset \cite{utkarsh} as the training set and a subset of \textit{ARXPHOTO314} \cite{arxnetsa:2025} as the drift identification set. 

The Landscape Recognition dataset consists of 12000 curated, web-scraped images across five categories (\texttt{Coast}, \texttt{Desert}, \texttt{Forest}, \texttt{Glacier}, \texttt{Mountain}). This serves as the reference distribution where the HASeparator learns its hyperspherical prototypes. 

The ARXPHOTO314 dataset comprises $18{,}320$ unconstrained, real-world mobile photographs, characterized by high variability in lighting, framing, and environmental conditions. We map the similar ones to the Landscapes's five classes as in Table~\ref{tab:classmap}. This target domain represents a real-world distributional shift where the model encounters geometrically ambiguous samples. Our prioritization pipeline exploits this shift by identifying \textit{high-interest samples} instances where the model’s learned boundaries are weakest and selecting them for targeted fine-tuning to recover accuracy under limited annotation budgets.

\subsection{Model Architectures and Training}
\label{sec:training}

Both models utilize a ResNet-50~\cite{he-zhang-etal:2016} as a common backbone trained from scratch, followed by a bottleneck module (\texttt{Embedder64}) that maps the
2048-dimensional global average-pooled feature to a
64-dimensional latent space via BatchNorm $\to$ Dropout$(0.3)$
$\to$ Linear$(2048, 64)$ $\to$ BatchNorm.
This 64-dimensional bottleneck mirrors the ResNet-50 variant
of the original HASeparator paper \cite{kansizoglou-santavas-etal:2020}
and provides a shared, comparable latent space for drift analysis
across both models.

\subsubsection{ResNet-50 as Baseline Model}

The baseline appends a linear classification head
$\mathrm{Linear}(64, C)$ to the bottleneck and trains with
categorical cross-entropy.
No angular or norm constraints are imposed on the embeddings.
Training uses Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with
momentum $\gamma = 0.9$, and
weight decay $\lambda = 10^{-4}$, with initial learning rate
$\eta_0 = 0.1$ decaying by a factor of $10$ at epochs
$\lfloor 0.6 \cdot T \rfloor$ and $\lfloor 0.8 \cdot T \rfloor$
(multi-step schedule, $T = 150$ epochs).

\subsubsection{HAS Model}

The HAS model replaces the linear head with a
\texttt{HASeparatorMultiClass} layer, which maintains an
$N \times C$ weight matrix on the unit sphere and computes the
composite loss~(\ref{eq:total_loss}).
Training employs SGD with the same momentum, weight decay, and
multi-step schedule as the baseline, but with a lower
initial learning rate $\eta_0 = 0.05$ to prevent early
geometric collapse under the angular penalty.
Hyperparameters are set to $\alpha = 1.0$, $\beta = 0.5$,
$m = 0.3$, and $\sigma = 8.0$, following empirical calibration
on the five-class landscape task.
The best-checkpoint strategy and batch size of $64$ are
identical to the baseline.

Training augmentation for both models consists of random
resized cropping to $224 \times 224$, random horizontal flipping,
and colour jitter (brightness and contrast $\pm 0.2$).
All evaluation and feature extraction are performed with a
deterministic standard transform (resize to $224\times224$,
ImageNet normalisation), ensuring that extracted embeddings are
unaffected by stochastic augmentation.


\subsection{Implementation Details}

Following training, both models are frozen and used in inference
mode to extract 64-dimensional embeddings for every sample in
the training set and the custom evaluation set as described in Section~\ref{sec:extraction}.
For the HAS model, the embedding $\hat{\be}_i \in \Sn^{63}$ is
the $\ell_2$-normalised bottleneck output, placing all HAS
representations on the unit 63-sphere by construction.
Baseline embeddings $\be_i \in \Rn^{64}$ carry no such constraint.
