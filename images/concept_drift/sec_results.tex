\subsection{Error Sensitivity Validation}
\label{sec:res_cifar}
We first validate \textbf{H1} by assessing the HAS-margin's sensitivity to label noise using the CIFAR-10N dataset. 
Table \ref{tab:cifar_sensitivity} demonstrates that the HAS-margin disproportionately flags human-annotated errors compared to confidence-based baselines. 

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \caption{Drift detection rates by human annotator agreement}
    \label{tab:cifar_sensitivity}
    \begin{tabular}{cccc}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Humans Wrong} & \textbf{Total} & \textbf{Baseline (\%)} & \textbf{HAS (\%)} \\
        \midrule
        0/3 & 29{,}896 & 0.66 & 3.04 \\
        1/3 & 14{,}777 & 1.06 & 4.03 \\
        2/3 &  4{,}260 & 1.57 & 6.46 \\
        3/3 &  1{,}067 & 3.19 & 11.81 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

As the consensus of human disagreement increases (from 0/3 to 3/3), the recall gap between the HAS-margin and the confidence-based baseline widens significantly. 
Specifically, for samples with total human disagreement (3/3), the HAS method achieves a recall of $11.81\%$, nearly 4 times higher than the $3.19\%$ achieved by the baseline. This confirms that our geometric margin $\Delta_i$ effectively acts as an automated, budget-efficient error detector, surfacing "high-interest" samples that are boundary-proximate and geometrically fragile, without requiring ground-truth labels during the selection process.

By observing Table~\ref{tab:direction} the most dominant drift direction is Forest toward Mountain, with $43.5\%$ of concept-drifted Forest samples exhibiting closest-boundary alignment with the Mountain class.
Sub-categories such as \emph{Pacific Island Forest}, \emph{Olive Tree
Cluster}, and \emph{Forest Panorama} share visual characteristics such as elevated terrain, and mixed textures, with Mountain sub-categories.
Subsequently, the second strongest pattern is Glacier toward Mountain ($28.8\%$).
In the category, sub-categories such as \emph{Ice Skating Rink} and \emph{Athletes on Snow
\& Ice} lack the natural glacial landscape features that the training set Glacier class exhibits, making Mountain the geometrically nearest alternative boundary.

Accordingly, a mutual pattern is observed between Coast and Desert, suggesting greater intra-class heterogeneity in the ARXPHOTO314 categories compared to the training dataset.

These directional patterns are entirely inaccessible to the baseline model, which produces only a scalar confidence value and cannot identify which decision boundary is being approached.




\subsection{Fine-Tuning Results}
\label{sec:res_finetune}

The data in Figure~\ref{fig:learning_curve} shows the classification accuracy on the test set as a function of annotation budget $N$ for both the HAS and baseline models, fine-tuned using
their respective drift-aware selection strategies.


\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \resizebox{0.8\textwidth}{!}{\input{../../results/learning_curve_study/learning_curve_study.pgf}}
    \caption{Learning curve from concept drift study.}
    \label{fig:learning_curve}
\end{figure}

The HAS model consistently outperforms the baseline across all tested budget levels ($N = 100$ to $N = 300$), with accuracy ranging from $58.3\%$ to $62.0\%$ for HAS and from $56.6\%$ to $61.5\%$ for the baseline.
Consistent with our initial hypothesis, the data demonstrate that the performance advantage of HAS is largest at small budgets.
At $N = 200$, the gap reaches $3.42$ percentage points ($61.4\%$ vs $57.9\%$), validating our hypothesis that targeted margin-based sample prioritisation is particularly valuable when annotation resources are scarce. As the budget grows toward $N = 300$, the gap narrows to $0.49$ percentage points, consistent with the expectation that at large budgets random selection eventually covers the most informative samples as well but also this further highlight that the severity drifted intances of the HAS methodology are better captured.


This convergence behaviour is corroborated by the
critical samples overlap column.
At $N = 100$, only $7$ instances are shared between the HAS and baseline selections,
confirming that the two strategies identify largely disjoint subsets at small budgets. At $N = 300$, the overlap grows to $48$ samples, reflecting the gradual convergence of the two selection sets as the budget approaches full pool coverage. Together, these results support hypothesis H2.
Thus, prioritising samples by their angular margin in HAS space yields systematically better
fine-tuning outcomes than random selection, with the benefit being most pronounced precisely when the annotation budget is most constrained.
Last but not least, we can observe that the HAS's accuracy is smoothly imporved but adding more critical instances in the fine-tuning pool. One the other hand the Baselines behavior does not follow a same smooth improvement behavior with spikes in accuracy at $120, 200, 240$ instances.
This further highlight that the severity drifted intances of the HAS methodology better captured.
