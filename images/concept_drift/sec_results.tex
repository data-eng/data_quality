\subsection{Error Sensitivity Validation}
\label{sec:res_cifar}
We first validate \textbf{H1} by assessing whether the HAS-margin
informativeness score is sensitive to genuine annotation error, using
CIFAR-10N. 
Thus, CIFAR-10N is used here specifically to test whether the informativeness score tracks
\emph{instance-level annotation difficulty}, independent of any
population-level shift signal, which is the cleanest possible setting for
validating H1 in isolation.

Table~\ref{tab:cifar_sensitivity} reports the fraction of each
human-agreement stratum flagged as informative at the standard operating
point $k=2$ in Equation~\ref{eq:thresh}.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \caption{Fraction of each human-agreement stratum flagged as informative ($k=2$).}
    \label{tab:cifar_sensitivity}
    \begin{tabular}{cccc}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Humans Wrong} & \textbf{Total} & \textbf{Baseline (\%)} & \textbf{HAS (\%)} \\
        \midrule
        0/3 & 29{,}896 & 0.64 & 2.56 \\
        1/3 & 14{,}777 & 0.91 & 3.40 \\
        2/3 &  4{,}260 & 1.50 & 5.59 \\
        3/3 &  1{,}067 & 3.19 & 10.22 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

As human-annotator disagreement increases from 0/3 to 3/3, the HAS-margin flag 
rate rises from $2.56\%$ to $10.22\%$ (a $7.66$ percentage-point
increase), compared to $0.64\%$ to $3.19\%$ for the confidence baseline 
(a $2.55$ point increase). At total disagreement (3/3), HAS flags
samples at $3.2\times$ the baseline rate. This confirms that the angular
margin is a substantially more sensitive, label-free indicator of
genuine annotation difficulty than softmax confidence.

Even within this same-image setting, the two scores disagree substantially 
at the instance level: $1{,}457$ images ($2.9\%$ of the
pool) are flagged as informative by the HAS-margin score while the model 
remains highly confident on them under the baseline score. These
are precisely the images a confidence-based selection strategy would fail 
to prioritize for relabelling: the model is not visibly uncertain
about them, yet their embeddings sit close to a decision boundary.

annotation budget $N$ for both models, each fine-tuned on an $N$-instance
subset selected as described in Section~\ref{sec:selection}: HAS selects
its subset by ranking the margin-flagged pool, while the Baseline draws
its subset uniformly at its own margin-flagged pool.

The HAS model consistently outperforms the baseline across all tested budget 
levels ($N = 100$ to $N = 300$), with accuracy ranging from $58.3\%$ to $62.0\%$ 
for HAS and from $56.6\%$ to $61.5\%$ for the baseline.
Consistent with our initial hypothesis, the data demonstrate that the performance 
advantage of HAS is largest at small budgets.
At $N = 200$, the gap reaches $3.42$ percentage points ($61.4\%$ vs $57.9\%$), 
validating our hypothesis that targeted margin-based sample prioritisation is 
particularly valuable when annotation resources are scarce. As the budget grows 
toward $N = 300$, the gap narrows to $0.49$ percentage points, consistent with 
the expectation that at large budgets Baseline's selection eventually covers the most 
informative samples as well but also this further highlight that the severity 
drifted intances of the HAS methodology are better captured.


This convergence behaviour is corroborated by the
critical samples overlap column.
At $N = 100$, only $7$ instances are shared between the HAS and baseline selections,
confirming that the two strategies identify largely disjoint subsets at small 
budgets. At $N = 300$, the overlap grows to $48$ samples, reflecting the 
gradual convergence of the two selection sets as the budget approaches full 
pool coverage. Together, these results support hypothesis H2. 
Thus, prioritising samples by their angular margin in HAS space yields 
systematically better 
fine-tuning outcomes than Baseline's space, with the benefit being most pronounced 
precisely when the annotation budget is most constrained.
Last but not least, we can observe that the HAS's accuracy is smoothly imporved 
but adding more critical instances in the fine-tuning pool. One the other hand 
the Baseline's behavior does not follow a same smooth improvement behavior with 
spikes in accuracy at $120, 200, 240$ instances.
This further highlight that the severity drifted intances of the HAS methodology 
better captured.

Lastly, in Figure~\ref{fig:same_instances} we show that the critical instances 
selected by HAS and Baseline are largely disjoint, with only $7$ shared instances 
at $N = 100$ and $48$ at $N = 300$. This further supports the conclusion that HAS 
identifies a more informative subset of samples for fine-tuning under limited 
annotation budgets.
