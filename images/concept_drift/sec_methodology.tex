
Our methodology shifts the focus from monolithic drift detection to the identification of informative samples—instances
that are geometrically ambiguous in the embedding space and thus critical for model adaptation. 
Unlike standard approaches that rely on output confidence, our approach leverages the hyperspherical geometry of the HASeparator. 
By monitoring the angular proximity to decision boundaries, we define a geometric signal that prioritizes samples prone to misclassification, 
effectively constructing a highly informative fine-tuning set under a limited annotation budget.

Since we have scoped our work as addressing gradual drift in the
meaning of existing labels, we assume that the labelled fine-tuning set should
include the instances that are further away from the semantic core of
our labels, since the instances at the periphery are the ones that
establish the decision boundaries. However, the periphery that we search for these informative-instances matter.

In the HASeparator embedding space, peripherality is naturally
quantified by the angular margin $\Delta_i$
(Equation~\ref{eq:margin}).
Instances with small $\Delta_i$ lie close to the separation hyperplane 
between their predicted class and the class with the closest decision boundary,
and are therefore the most geometrically fragile under gradual drift. The training margin
distribution $\{\Delta_i^{\mathrm{train}}\}$ defines what constitutes a central versus peripheral instance on
$\mathcal{S}^{63}$.
Central instances have large margins, deep within their class region, while peripheral instances
have small margins, near the decision boundaries.

In this context we hypothesize that instances located at the periphery of the semantic class 
regions are the primary drivers of model confusion, and thus, they constitute the most informative subset for fine-tuning:

\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textbf{H1 (Error Sensitivity):}] Margin-peripheral instances are significantly more prone to misclassification. We validate this using the CIFAR-10N dataset~\cite{} by analyzing the HAS-margin. We, therefore, demonstrate a high correlation with human-annotated noise labels, confirming its role as a robust error detector.
  \item[\textbf{H2 (Prioritization Efficiency):}] Using the HAS geometry to select peripheral instances yields a more informative fine-tuning set than similar methods in a simple ResNet-50 within the original space, leading to superior accuracy recovery.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Feature Extraction and Geometric Fragility Computation}
\label{sec:extraction}

Our prioritization pipeline operates on the frozen embeddings of the trained HAS model, deriving geometric signals that quantify the ambiguity and fragility of individual samples. These signals are computed post-hoc, independently of the HASeparator training hyperparameters ($\mu$ and $\sigma$), as they arise directly from the hyperspherical structure of the embedding space.

\subsubsection{Geometric Fragility Signal: Angular Margin}
\label{sec:margin}

To quantify geometric fragility, we utilize the angular margin $\Delta_i$. Let $\hat{e}_i$ be the unit-normalized embedding of sample $i$, and let $\hat{w}_{\hat{y}_i}$ and $\hat{w}_{\hat{y}_i^{(2)}}$ represent the unit-normalized weight vectors (prototypes) of the predicted class and the runner-up class, respectively. The runner-up class is defined as the class with the second-highest cosine similarity to the embedding, representing the decision boundary the model is most "tempted" to cross. We compute the angular margin as:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_i \;=\; (\hat{e}_i \cdot \hat{w}_{\hat{y}_i}) - (\hat{e}_i \cdot \hat{w}_{\hat{y}_i^{(2)}})\,.
  \label{eq:margin}
\end{equation}
This margin $\Delta_i$ corresponds to the angular distance between the embedding and the nearest class separation hyperplane on $\mathcal{S}^{63}$. 

A large $\Delta_i$ signifies that the embedding resides deep within the predicted class region, far from any decision boundary. Conversely, a small $\Delta_i$ identifies \textit{high-interest samples} that lie in close proximity to the separation hyperplane. When $\Delta_i \approx 0$, the sample sits on the boundary, and a negative $\Delta_i$ indicates that the runner-up class is more aligned with the embedding than the predicted class—a strong indicator of potential misclassification.

We prioritize these \textit{high-interest samples} by selecting instances with the smallest positive $\Delta_i$. Specifically, samples falling below the threshold $\tau_\Delta$ are selected for human annotation:
\begin{equation}
  \tau_\Delta \;=\; \max\!\bigl(\mu_\Delta - k\,\sigma_\Delta,\ 0\bigr),
  \label{eq:margin_thresh}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_\Delta$ and $\sigma_\Delta$ are the mean and standard deviation of the training margin distribution, and $k=2.0$ acts as our sensitivity parameter. This geometric criterion effectively isolates samples that are boundary-proximate, and thus geometrically fragile, without requiring explicit ground-truth labels.

\subsubsection{ResNet-50 Reference Statistics}
While our core method utilizes the angular margin, we maintain the ResNet-50 output certainty as a secondary heuristic. The ResNet-50  confidence $q_i = \max_j\, p_{ij}$ serves as a standard metric to assess model output certainty. However, in our comparative analysis, we show that the geometric signals derived from $\Delta_i$ offer superior sensitivity for identifying samples that the baseline model misclassifies with high confidence—samples that are "hidden" from standard drift detection pipelines.
