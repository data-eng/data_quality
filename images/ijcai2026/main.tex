\section{Introduction}

% Positioning. Background.
Sustainable AI has emerged in an era where AI workload enormously increase the data center emissions~\cite{jha-etal:2025}. 
Computationally expensive and time-consuming neural networks are being trained daily with massive datasets, while the demand for even larger, better or more task-oriented datasets shows no sign of slowing down.
Questiong like: "How much more data do I need~\cite{mahmood-etal:2022}?" and "do we need more training data~\cite{zhu-vondrick-etal:2016}?" highlight that large-scale datasets have increasingly become a contraint rathen than a beneficial resource. 


In the context of dataset reduction, two main research directions have emerged:
\emph{coreset selection} and \emph{dataset distillation}. 
Coreset methods work directly on real samples and aim to pick a small subset
that preserves the information characteristics of the full dataset, typically by
optimizing geometry, or gradient similarity.
On the other hand, distillation~\cite{lei-tao:2024} synthesizes a tiny set of artificial dataset whose training
dynamics mimic those of the original dataset, often using generative models or 
trajectory matching in pixel or latent space.

Recent work~\cite{moser-shanbhag-etal:202} has highlighted several structural limitations of existing coreset methods.
Training-free, geometry-based approaches such as Herding~\cite{welling:2009} or 
$k$-Center Greedy~\cite{sener-savarese:2017} crucially depend on the quality of the feature extractor. 
If the embedding does not faithfully capture the class structure, selection tends to pick arbitrary
points with good geometric coverage but poor alignment with the final decision boundary.
Training-oriented approaches such as CAL~\cite{margatina-vernikos-barrault:2021} and 
DeepFool~\cite{ducoffe-precioso:2018} mitigate
this by exploiting training dynamics with decision boundary based methodologies, 
but with a high cost at runtimes that makes them difficult at scale.


Lastly, compared to the dataset distillation, coreset selection is applied to a 
various domains.
It has been proven valuable for imbalanced datasets~\cite{sivasubramanian-aagalapatti-etal:2024,luo-zhao-etal:2024}, continual learning approaches~\cite{titsias-schwarz-etal:2019,mirzadeh-farajtabar-etal:2020}, 
machine unlearning~\cite{bourtoule-chandrasekaran-etal:2021} or even large languare models~\cite{killamsetty-zhao-etal:2021} and self-supervised approaches~\cite{kim-bae-yun:2023,singh-vashishtha-etal:2025}, further emphasizing the importance of effective and efficienct coreset selection methodologies.



% In this work, we take a different stance and investigate whether sustainable AI can be advanced through coreset selection rather than dataset synthesis. 
We argue that it is feasible to capture specific instances, within a training dataset, that are the most informative ones and preserve the networks decision information, with a lightweight approach, resulting faster and thus more energy efficient training.
We analyze the trade-off between subset size, accuracy, and training cost and show that carefully selected coreset can rival large-scale datasets in termes of accuracy. 


In summary the contributions of this works are:
\begin{itemize}
	\item a latent-space discrimination framework that leverages a pretrained diffusion model as a frozen encoder,
	\item a lightweight weigth embedder with a hyperplane assigned softmax head, ensuring class discriminative feature vectors,
	\item a cosine fartherst-first coreset selection approach for the discriminative feature space,
	\item an extensive experimentation on multiple networks, highlight the computational costs of different approaches for the coreset selection.
\end{itemize}


\section{Method}

Data distillation, data selection and, in fact, most modern ANN
approaches follow a general pattern where the raw data is first mapped
to a learned latent representation that reduces the dimensionality of
the input. This approach offers the great benefit of mapping the data
to a more informative, compressed space where the transformations
applied by subsequent network layers can be more effectively learned.
It also offers the computational benefit that such latent representations
can be learned in an unsupervised manner, making them re-usable across 
learning tasks over the same kind of data.

The work described here is positioned as follows with respect to the main
body of literature in dataset reduction:
%
\begin{itemize}

\item Data distilation: There should be no need to synthesise
  characteristic instances in a large dataset, we hypothesise that
  such a dataset already has the characteristic instances we need if
  we can select them,
\item Core set selection: Aiming for the most characteristic instances
  of each class will achieve the bulk of the accuracy, but is likely
  to miss nuanced distinctions. We hypothesise that selection should
  be driven by the difficulty or complexity of the distinctions that
  need to be learned, or, in other words, that selection should be
  biased not towards the most characteristic, central instances but
  towards the `difficult' cases, the ones that define the borders
  between classes.
\end{itemize}
%
Putting these two together, we hypothesise that a selection method can
be as effective as distilation (but at a lower computational cost),
and that the best strategy is to make a selection that is denser at
the periphery of a class than at its core.

To test this hypothesis we developed our three-step method for
selecting the most informative images to use for an image
classification task:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item Projection to latent space
\item Further projection to contrastive space
\item Instance selection
\end{itemize}

The first step is to project the input images to an informative,
compressed latent space, using a pretrained \emph{variational
autoencoder (VAE)}, specifically a Stable Diffusion (SD)
VAE~\cite{fotiadis-valencia-etal:2022,rombach-blattmann-etal:2021}
pretrained on the COCO dataset. The dimensionality of the latent space
is $(Bx4x(H/8)x(W/8))$, where $B$ the batch size, $4$ the number of
channels, $H$ the image height and $W$ the image width.

Although autoencoders preserve the important characteristics of each
sample, the resulting space is not expected to meaningfully represent
the distinction between class-characteristic and class-peripheral
instances; For such a distinction a \emph{contrastive} space is needed.
We achieve this by training a lightweight embedding network coupled
with a discriminative hyperplane-assisted softmax
head~\cite{kansizoglou-santavas-etal:2020} to obtain inter-class
discrimination and intra-class compactness. Note that the VAE is
frozen during this training.

% UNCLEAR, TBD
Feature discrimination is an important challenge in computer vision.
It is important that embeddings exhibit high discrimination along
different classes as well as intra-class compactness. Towards that,
Kansizoglou et al. [\citeyear{kansizoglou-santavas-etal:2020}]
proposed a class hyperplane-based segregation on the contrary of the
common class centers separation scheme.


In our implementation, the HASeparator layer receives an embedding
vector $\mathbf{e}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and a learnable weight matrix
$W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times C}$, with one column per class. Both the
embeddings and weights are $\ell_2$–normalized, so that
$\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_i = \mathbf{e}_i / \|\mathbf{e}_i\|_2$ and
$\tilde{W}$ has unit-norm columns. The classification logits are then
given by a scaled cosine similarity
\[
\mathbf{o}_i = s \, \tilde{\mathbf{e}}_i^\top \tilde{W},
\]
where $s$ is the scale factor. 

The key difference of HASeparator lies in an additional penalty term
defined with respect to the separation hyperplanes of the target class.
For a sample with label $y$, the normal vector of the hyperplane
separating class $y$ from class $j$ is proportional to
$\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_y - \tilde{\mathbf{w}}_j$. 
After normalization we obtain unit normals $\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{y,j}$, and we compute the
projection of the normalized embedding:
\[
p_{i,j} = \tilde{\mathbf{e}}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf{h}}_{y,j}.
\]
Intuitively, large $p_{i,j}$ means that $\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_i$ is far
from the decision boundary between $y$ and $j$, while small $p_{i,j}$ 
means it lies close to that boundary. 
HASeparator introduces a margin parameter $m \in (0,1]$ and penalizes 
projections that fall below this margin with
\[
J(p_{i,j}) = m - \min(p_{i,j}, m) \;\; \ge 0,
\]
so that embeddings are explicitly pushed away from all hyperplanes that
separate their target class from the others. 
The total penalty for a sample is obtained by summing $J(p_{i,j})$ over all classes $j$, 
and the final training objective is the standard cross-entropy on
$\mathbf{o}_i$ plus the batch-averaged hyperplane penalty.

Compared to centre-based margin losses, which implicitly assume that 
the last-layer weights approximate class centres, HASeparator works
directly with the geometry of the decision boundaries, enforcing large
angular gaps between classes while preserving intra-class compactness.
In our setting, we use the normalized embeddings output by this layer
as the contrastive feature space on which cosine $k$-center coreset
selection is performed. As a result, our instance selection operates in
a space where class borders are explicitly “pushed apart”, making
boundary samples more clearly identifiable and better aligned with our
goal of biasing the coreset towards difficult, classes boundary
instances.


%We hypothesize that the representative instances i.e the ones closest to the classes centroid are not informed of the classes true extend and even more so of the nuances of the boundaries between this class and the other classes.
Our selection scheme is inspired by the clustering algorithm of
Doshi et al.~[\citeyear{doshi-rinal-bhadka-etal:2013}],
a fast and greedy algorithm to select k points from a cluster.
The algorithm assumes the distance between an instance and its closest
previously-selected instance to indicate how well the instance is
represented by the current selection. The algorithm first selects the
centroid of the data, then selects the least-represented instance
(effectively the one furthest away from the centroid), and the
proceeds to select the instance that least represented by the two
previously selected intances (the one furthest away from its
representative, its closest previously-selected instance), and so on.
Since the instances across the area occupied by the class
will be furthest away from all previously selected instances, this
selection strategy will first bounce across the periphery and then
will sample the inner parts of the class area, with lower preference
to the core where distances are smaller.
%
Following Khan et al.~[\citeyear{khan-kafi-sarker-etal:2021}]
we utilize cosine similarity as our distance measure in the embedding space.
In practice, we work with $\ell_2$-normalized embeddings and apply
this farthest-first selection with cosine distance, which yields a
class-aware coreset selection method biased to class's borders in the learned
contrastive space.


The ConvNet embedder operates directly on VAE latents $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times (H/8) \times (W/8)}$ and is
deliberately kept small to reduce computational cost while preserving discriminative power. 
Its building block is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:conv_block}.
The full ConvNet architecture is depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:convnet}. 
A $4$-channel input latent first passes through a input layers consisting of a 2-D convolution, GroupNorm and SiLU activation.
The resulting feature map is processed by three residual blocks as defined above, all at the same spatial resolution.
Finally, we apply global average pooling over the spatial dimensions, flatten the pooled features, and use a linear
layer to project them to a $d$-dimensional embedding vector $\mathbf{f} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$.
This vector is then fed into the HASeparator head, which produces the normalized embedding 
$\mathbf{z}'$ used for coreset selection.
Overall, this ConvNet transfers  the frozen VAE latent space to a constrastive feature space, 
transforming features into a discriminative contrastive space that is well suited for our cosine $k$-center coreset selection.

\section{Experiments and Results}

Related work.
%here add the the GPU of the hercules

All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA H100 NVL GPU.
For CIFAR-10 we train each model for 250 epochs. %, while for MNIST we train for 50 epochs. 
Additionally, we use AdamW with a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-3}$ and weight decay $1 \times 10^{-4}$. 
For coreset subset selection, we select instances at subsets from $10\%$ to $90\%$ in steps of $10\%$, as well as smaller subsets of $0.1\%$,$0.5\%$, $1\%$ and $5\%$.
Moreover, coresets are selected from the latent feature space of our ConvNet embedder trained 
on top of the frozen Stable Diffusion VAE.
We first compute L2-normalized embeddings for all training points and then apply the class-aware cosine $k$-center selection, 
yielding one coreset per target data subset.
These coresets are then used across all subsequent experiments, including the model-agnostic evaluation.


Initially, we compared our method against a broad set of existing data selection 
and coreset approaches, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:performance_data}.
All methods are trained from scratch on CIFAR-10 using ResNet-18 as presented in~\cite{moser-shanbhag-etal:2025}, 
and we report the test accuracy ranging from extremely small subsets (0.1\%, 0.5\%, 1\%) up to the full dataset (100\%).

In the very small data subsets (0.1–1\%), our approach clearly outperforms all baselines, 
achieving 32.74\%, 46.92\%, and 52.11\% test accuracy at 0.1\%, 0.5\%, and 1\% of the data, 
respectively, while the next-best methods remain below 25–43\%. 
At 10\% of the data, our method also achieves the highest accuracy (79.21\%), 
and at 20\% it remains competitive with the strongest baselines. 
For larger data fractions (30–100\%), all methods converge to very similar performance, 
as expected when most of the training set is available. 
Overall, this benchmark indicates that our coreset selection strategy is particularly 
effective in the smaller subsets, where subset quality matters the most, further highlighting our hypothesis 
that the most informative instances are the ones at the classed boarders.



Futhermore, to assess whether our coreset selection is model-agnostic, we train a range of standard 
baseline architectures \emph{from scratch} on each selected subset, without using any pretrained model. 
The resulting test accuracies for ConvNet, ResNet-18/50/101, and DenseNet-169 are reported 
in Table~\ref{tab:model_agnostic}. 
These results show that the same coreset, transfers well to substantially larger backbones, which achieve high accuracy.
For example, with only 20\% of the data, ResNet-18 already reaches 86.22\% test accuracy and 
DenseNet-169 reaches 86.48\%, while with 90\% of the data all large backbones exceed 94\% accuracy.
This suggests that our selection procedure is not overfitting to the specific embedder used for coreset construction, 
but instead identifies genuinely informative instances for the classification task.

Additionally, to quantify the computational cost associated with these baselines, Table~\ref{tab:trainable_parameters} 
reports the number of trainable parameters for each architecture, while Table~\ref{tab:training_time} 
summarizes the end-to-end training time (in minutes) on CIFAR-10 for each model and data subset. 
Together, these tables highlight that larger models (e.g., ResNet-101, DenseNet-169) benefit more from 
our coreset selection in terms of final accuracy at the larger subsets, but at the price of significantly 
increased training time and trainable parameters compared to our custom ConvNet.


To further evaluate or coreset setup effectiveness, Table~\ref{tab:conv_vs_res_emb} compares two coreset selection setups. 
In the first case, we train our latent ConvNet embedder and use its feature space on
which cosine $k$-center selection is performed. 
Each subset (from 10\% to 90\% of the training data) is then used to train a classifier with the same ConvNet. 
In the second case, we repeat the entire procedure using a ResNet-18 as latent embedder instead.

Across all data coresets, the ConvNet selected coreset consistently yields substantially 
higher test accuracy than the ResNet-18-based one (e.g., 53.09\% vs.\ 39.46\% at 10\%,
and 76.32\% vs.\ 44.20\% at 90\%). 
This suggests that, under a fixed training coreset, a deeper embedder does not 
automatically provide a better feature space for coreset selection.
Thus, ResNet-18 appears undertrained in this setting and produces less useful embeddings 
making coreset selection less efficient. 
In practice, a compact latent ConvNet can be a more effective and computationally efficient 
choice for coreset selection.
