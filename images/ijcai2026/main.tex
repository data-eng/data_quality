\section{Method}

Our three-step method for selecting which images to use in a
downstream machine learning task proceeds as follows:
%
\begin{enumerate}
\item Projection to unsuervised latent space
\item Further projection to supervised contrastive space
\item Instance sampling
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Instance representation}

% https://huggingface.co/stabilityai/sd-vae-ft-mse-original
% https://github.com/Spijkervet/SimCLR/

The first step is to project the input images to an informative,
compressed latent space, using a pretrained
\emph{variational autoencoder (VAE)}.
Specifically, we use the \emph{CompVis Stable Diffusion} VAE
trained on MS-COCO \cite{rombach-blattmann-etal:2021}
as augemented by Fotiadis et al.~\cite{fotiadis-valencia-etal:2022}.
The dimensionality of the latent space is
$(4 \times (H/8) \times (W/8))$, where $4$ is the number of channels
and $H \times W$ the image dimensions.

Although autoencoders preserve the important characteristics of each
sample, the resulting space is not expected to meaningfully represent
the distinction between class-characteristic and class-peripheral
instances; For such a distinction we must construct a \emph{contrastive}
space that gives inter-class discrimination and intra-class
compactness. We achieve this by the supervised training of a
lightweight embedding network, positioned after the layers
of the (frozen) VAE. Specifically, we use
the \emph{HASeparator layer} \cite{kansizoglou-santavas-etal:2020}
which uses a discriminative \emph{hyperplane-assisted} softmax head to
give hyperplane-based separation instead of the (more commonly used)
class-center separation.

In our implementation, the HASeparator layer receives an embedding
vector $\mathbf{e}_i \in \mathbf{R}^d$ and a learnable weight matrix
$W \in \mathbf{R}^{d \times C}$, with one column per class. Both the
embeddings and weights are $\ell_2$–normalized, so that
$\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_i = \mathbf{e}_i / \|\mathbf{e}_i\|_2$ and
$\tilde{W}$ has unit-norm columns. The classification logits are then
given by a scaled cosine similarity
\[
\mathbf{o}_i = s \, \tilde{\mathbf{e}}_i^\top \tilde{W},
\]
where $s$ is the scale factor. 

The key difference of HASeparator lies in an additional penalty term
defined with respect to the separation hyperplanes of the target class.
For a sample with label $y$, the normal vector of the hyperplane
separating class $y$ from class $j$ is proportional to
$\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_y - \tilde{\mathbf{w}}_j$. 
After normalization we obtain unit normals $\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{y,j}$, and we compute the
projection of the normalized embedding:
\[
p_{i,j} = \tilde{\mathbf{e}}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf{h}}_{y,j}.
\]
Intuitively, large $p_{i,j}$ means that $\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_i$ is far
from the decision boundary between $y$ and $j$, while small $p_{i,j}$ 
means it lies close to that boundary. 
HASeparator introduces a margin parameter $m \in (0,1]$ and penalizes 
projections that fall below this margin with
\[
J(p_{i,j}) = m - \min(p_{i,j}, m) \;\; \ge 0,
\]
so that embeddings are explicitly pushed away from all hyperplanes that
separate their target class from the others. 
The total penalty for a sample is obtained by summing $J(p_{i,j})$ over all classes $j$, 
and the final training objective is the standard cross-entropy on
$\mathbf{o}_i$ plus the batch-averaged hyperplane penalty.

Compared to centre-based margin losses, which implicitly assume that 
the last-layer weights approximate class centres, HASeparator works
directly with the geometry of the decision boundaries to enforce large
angular gaps between classes while preserving intra-class compactness.
In our setting, we use the normalized embeddings output by this layer
as the contrastive feature space on which cosine $k$-center coreset
selection is performed. As a result, our instance selection operates in
a space where class borders are explicitly \quoted{pushed apart}, making
boundary samples more clearly identifiable and better aligned with our
goal of biasing the coreset towards difficult, classes boundary
instances.

The ConvNet embedder operates directly on VAE latents $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbf{R}^{4 \times (H/8) \times (W/8)}$ and is
deliberately kept small to reduce computational cost while preserving discriminative power. 
Its building block is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:conv_block}.
The full ConvNet architecture is depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:convnet}. 
A $4$-channel input latent first passes through a input layers consisting of a 2-D convolution, GroupNorm and SiLU activation.
The resulting feature map is processed by three residual blocks as defined above, all at the same spatial resolution.
Finally, we apply global average pooling over the spatial dimensions, flatten the pooled features, and use a linear
layer to project them to a $d$-dimensional embedding vector $\mathbf{f} \in \mathbf{R}^{d}$.
This vector is then fed into the HASeparator head, which produces the normalized embedding 
$\mathbf{z}'$ used for coreset selection.
Overall, this ConvNet transfers  the frozen VAE latent space to a constrastive feature space, 
transforming features into a discriminative contrastive space that is well suited for our cosine $k$-center coreset selection.

\subsection{Instance sampling}


%We hypothesize that the representative instances i.e the ones closest to the classes centroid are not informed of the classes true extend and even more so of the nuances of the boundaries between this class and the other classes.

Our selection scheme is inspired by the clustering algorithm of
Doshi et al.~[\citeyear{doshi-rinal-bhadka-etal:2013}],
a fast and greedy algorithm to select k points from a cluster.
A core concept in this algorithm is that of an instance's
\emph{representative:} Assume a subset $S$ of the overall set of
instances; Then for any instance $i$ \emph{not} in $S$, we shall call
the representative of $i$ the member of $S$ that has the smallest
distance from $i$. The Doshi et al. algorithm uses this concept
to measure how well a new instance is represented by each of the
current clusters. It then selects the worst-represented instance to
seed a new cluster, until the expected number of clusters has been
reached.

Our adaptation of this algorithm re-uses the concept of the
\emph{representative} from the Doshi et al. algorithm to ensure that
the instances that get added to the core set are those that are
not well-represented in the current core set. In more detail,
our sampling method proceeds as follows:
%
\begin{enumerate}
\item Find the centroid of the class. Add it to the core set.
\item For all instances not in the core set, find the distance to
  their representative in the core set.
\item Add the worst-represented instance to the core set.
\item Repeat from Step 2 until the user-specified core-set size has
  been reached.
\end{enumerate}
%
This algorithm will first select an outlying instance, the one
furthest away from the centroid, since in the first iteration all
instances are represented by the centroid. It will then select an
instance roughly diametrically opposite to the first instance, since
that will be furthest away from its representative (the centroid or
the first instance); and will proceed bouncing around the periphery of
the class until the periphery has become sampled densely enough that
samples deeper inside the class area are less well-represented.

Following Khan et al.~[\citeyear{khan-kafi-sarker-etal:2021}]
we utilize cosine similarity as our distance measure in the embedding space.
In practice, we work with $\ell_2$-normalized embeddings and apply
this farthest-first selection with cosine distance, which yields a
class-aware coreset selection method biased to class's borders in the learned
contrastive space.

\section{Experiments and Results}

%All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA H100 NVL GPU.

For CIFAR-10 we train each model for 250 epochs. %, while for MNIST we train for 50 epochs. 
Additionally, we use AdamW with a learning rate of $2E-3$ and weight decay $1E-4$. 
For coreset subset selection, we select instances at subsets from $10\%$ to $90\%$ in steps of $10\%$, as well as smaller subsets of $0.1\%$,$0.5\%$, $1\%$ and $5\%$.
Moreover, coresets are selected from the latent feature space of our ConvNet embedder trained 
on top of the frozen Stable Diffusion VAE.
We first compute L2-normalized embeddings for all training points and then apply the class-aware cosine $k$-center selection, 
yielding one coreset per target data subset.
These coresets are then used across all subsequent experiments, including the model-agnostic evaluation.


Initially, we compared our method against a broad set of existing data selection 
and coreset approaches, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:performance_data}.
All methods are trained from scratch on CIFAR-10 using ResNet-18 as presented in~\cite{moser-shanbhag-etal:2025}, 
and we report the test accuracy ranging from extremely small subsets (0.1\%, 0.5\%, 1\%) up to the full dataset (100\%).

In the very small data subsets (0.1–1\%), our approach clearly outperforms all baselines, 
achieving 32.74\%, 46.92\%, and 52.11\% test accuracy at 0.1\%, 0.5\%, and 1\% of the data, 
respectively, while the next-best methods remain below 25–43\%. 
At 10\% of the data, our method also achieves the highest accuracy (79.21\%), 
and at 20\% it remains competitive with the strongest baselines. 
For larger data fractions (30–100\%), all methods converge to very similar performance, 
as expected when most of the training set is available. 
Overall, this benchmark indicates that our coreset selection strategy is particularly 
effective in the smaller subsets, where subset quality matters the most, further highlighting our hypothesis 
that the most informative instances are the ones at the class boarders.


Futhermore, to assess whether our coreset selection is model-agnostic, we train a range of standard 
baseline architectures \emph{from scratch} on each selected subset, without using any pretrained model. 
The resulting test accuracies for ConvNet, ResNet-18/50/101, and DenseNet-169 are reported 
in Table~\ref{tab:model_agnostic}. 
These results show that the same coreset, transfers well to substantially larger backbones, which achieve high accuracy.
For example, with only 20\% of the data, ResNet-18 already reaches 86.22\% test accuracy and 
DenseNet-169 reaches 86.48\%, while with 90\% of the data all large backbones exceed 94\% accuracy.
This suggests that our selection procedure is not overfitting to the specific embedder used for coreset construction, 
but instead identifies genuinely informative instances for the classification task.

Additionally, to quantify the computational cost associated with these baselines, Table~\ref{tab:trainable_parameters} 
reports the number of trainable parameters for each architecture, while Table~\ref{tab:training_time} 
summarizes the end-to-end training time (in minutes) on CIFAR-10 for each model and data subset. 
Together, these tables highlight that larger models (e.g., ResNet-101, DenseNet-169) benefit more from 
our coreset selection in terms of final accuracy at the larger subsets, but at the price of significantly 
increased training time and trainable parameters compared to our custom ConvNet.


To further evaluate or coreset setup effectiveness, Table~\ref{tab:conv_vs_res_emb} compares two coreset selection setups. 
In the first case, we train our latent ConvNet embedder and use its feature space on
which cosine $k$-center selection is performed. 
Each subset (from 10\% to 90\% of the training data) is then used to train a classifier with the same ConvNet. 
In the second case, we repeat the entire procedure using a ResNet-18 as latent embedder instead.

Across all data coresets, the ConvNet selected coreset consistently yields substantially 
higher test accuracy than the ResNet-18-based one (e.g., 53.09\% vs.\ 39.46\% at 10\%,
and 76.32\% vs.\ 44.20\% at 90\%). 
This suggests that, under a fixed training coreset, a deeper embedder does not 
automatically provide a better feature space for coreset selection.
Thus, ResNet-18 appears undertrained in this setting and produces less useful embeddings 
making coreset selection less efficient. 
In practice, a compact latent ConvNet can be a more effective and computationally efficient 
choice for coreset selection.
