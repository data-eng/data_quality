\section{Introduction}

% Positioning. Background.
Sustainable AI has emerged in an era where AI workload enormously increase the data center emissions~\cite{jha-etal:2025}. 
Computationally expensive and time-consuming neural networks are being trained daily with massive datasets, while the demand for even larger, better or more task-oriented datasets shows no sign of slowing down.
Questiong like: "How much more data do I need~\cite{mahmood-etal:2022}?" and "do we need more training data~\cite{zhu-vondrick-etal:2016}?" highlight that large-scale datasets have increasingly become a contraint rathen than a beneficial resource. 

% Here about DD
Towards that, dataset distillation (DD) has been proposed as a remedy~\cite{lei-tao:2024}. 
DD aprroaches synthesize smaller synthetic datasets that preserve the important representation characteristics of their original counterpart and at the same time achieve similar results. 
However these methods still generate new data in an ecosystem already burdened by data overload and significant computational cost.


In this work, we take a different stance and investigate whether sustainable AI can be advanced through coreset selection rather than dataset synthesis. 
We argue that it is feasible to capture specific instances, within a training dataset, that are the most informative ones and preserve the networks decision information, resulting faster and thus more energy efficient training.
We analyze the trade-off between subset size, accuracy, and training cost and show that carefully selected coreset can rival large-scale datasets in termes of accuracy. 
Moreover, trought extensive experimentation we outline that model's accuracy does not necessarly depict the quality of the coreset, however model's accuracy is higly assosiated with the models properties. 

In summary the contributions of this works are:
\begin{itemize}
	\item a latent-space discrimination framework that leverages a pretrained diffusion model as a frozen encoder,
	\item a lightweight weigth embedder with a hyperplane assigned softmax head, ensuring class discriminative feature vectors,
	\item a cosine fartherst-first coreset selection approach for the discriminative feature space,
	\item an extensive experimentation on multiple networks, highlight the computational costs of different approaches for the coreset selection.
\end{itemize}


\section{Method}

Data distillation, data selection and, in fact, most modern ANN
approaches follow a general pattern where the raw data is first mapped
to a learned latent representation that reduces the dimensionality of
the input. This approach offers the great benefit of mapping the data
to a more informative, compressed space where the transformations
applied by subsequent network layers can be more effectively learned.
It also the offers the computational benefit that such latent representations
can be learned in an unsupervised manner, making them re-usable across 
learning tasks over the same kind of data.

The work described here is positioned as follows with respect to the main
body of literature in dataset reduction:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item Data distilation: There should be no neeed to synthesise
  characteristic instances in a large dataset, we hypothesise that
  such a dataset already has the characteristic instances we need if
  we can select them,
\item Core set selection: Aiming for the most characteristic instances
  of each class will achieve the bulk of the accuracy, but is likely
  to miss nuanced distinctions. We hypothesise that selection should
  be driven by the difficulty or complexity of the distinctions that
  need to be learned, or, in other words, that selection should be
  biased not towards the most characteristic, central instances but
  towards the `difficult' cases, the ones that define the borders
  between classes.
\end{itemize}
%
Putting these two together, we hypothesise that a selection method can
be as effective as distilation (but at a lower computational cost),
and that the best strategy is to make a selection that is denser at
the periphery of a class than at its core.

To test this hypothesis we developed our three-step method for
selecting the most informative images to use for an image
classification task:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item Projection to latent space
\item Further projection to contrastive space
\item Instance selection
\end{itemize}

The first step is to project the input images to an informative,
compressed latent space, using a pretrained \emph{variational
autoencoder (VAE)}, specifically a Stable Diffusion (SD)
VAE~\cite{fotiadis-valencia-etal:2022,rombach-blattmann-etal:2021}
pretrained on the COCO dataset. The dimensionality of the latent space
is $(Bx4x(H/8)x(W/8))$, where $B$ the batch size, $4$ the number of
channels, $H$ the image height and $W$ the image width.

Although autoencoders preserve the important characteristics of each
sample, the resulting space is not expected to meaningfully represent
the distinction between class-characteristic and class-peripheral
instances; For such a distinction a \emph{contrastive} space is needed.
We achieve this by training a lightweight embedding network coupled
with a discriminative hyperplane-assisted softmax
head~\cite{kansizoglou2020haseparator} to obtain inter-class
discrimination and intra-class compactness. Note that the VAE is
frozen during this training.

% UNCLEAR, TBD
Feature discrimination is an important challenge in computer vision.
It is important that embeddings exhibit high discrimination along
different classes as well as intra-class compactness.  Towards that,
Kansizoglou et al. [\citeyear{kansizoglou-santavas-etal:2020}]
proposed a class hyperplane-based segregation on the contrary of the
common class centers separation scheme.

These discriminative embeddings are then used as the feature space on
which we apply our cosine farthest-first coreset selection procedure,
which focuses on selecting informative and boundary-biased
representatives for each class.

%We hypothesize that the representative instances i.e the ones closest to the classes centroid are not informed of the classes true extend and even more so of the nuances of the boundaries between this class and the other classes.

This is inspired by the clustering algorithm by
Doshi et al.~[\citeyear{doshi-rinal-bhadka-etal:2013}],
a fast and greedy algorithm to select k points from a cluster.
The algorithm assumes the distance between an instance and its closest
previously-selected instance to indicate how well the instance is
represented by the current selection. The algorithm first selects the
centroid of the data, then selects the least-represented instance
(effectively the one furthest away from the centroid), and the
proceeds to select the instance that least represented by the two
previously selected intances (the one furthest away from its
representative, its closest previously-selected instance), and so on.
Since the instances across the area occupied by the class
will be furthest away from all previously selected instances, this
selection strategy will first bounce across the periphery and then
will sample the inner parts of the class area, with lower preference
to the core where distances are smaller.
%
Following Khan et al.~[\citeyear{khan-kafi-sarker-etal:2021}]
we utilize cosine similarity as the distance metric.


\section{Experiments and Results}

Related work.
%here add the the GPU of the hercules

We run all experiments on a GPU. 
For CIFAR-10 we train each model for 250 epochs, while for MNIST we train for 50 epochs. 
Additionally, we use AdamW with a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-3}$ and weight decay $1 \times 10^{-4}$. 
For coreset subset selection, we select instances at subsets from $10\%$ to $90\%$ in steps of $10\%$.

To assess whether our coreset selection is model-agnostic, we first compute a single set of coresets on CIFAR-10 using our latent ConvNet embedder and cosine $k$-center selection. 
Afterward, we discard the VAE and embedder and train a range of standard baseline architectures \emph{from scratch} on each selected subset, without using any pretrained model. 
The resulting test accuracies for ConvNet, ResNet-18/50/101, and DenseNet-169 are reported in Table~\ref{tab:model_agnostic}. 
These results show that the same coreset, chosen in the latent ConvNet feature space, transfers well to substantially larger backbones, which achieve high accuracy even at small coresets.

To quantify the computational cost associated with these baselines, Table~\ref{tab:trainable_parameters} reports the number of trainable parameters for each architecture, while Table~\ref{tab:training_time} summarizes the end-to-end training time (in minutes) on CIFAR-10 for each model and data subset. 
Together, these tables highlight that larger models (e.g., ResNet-101, DenseNet-169) benefit more from our coreset selection in terms of final accuracy, but at the price of significantly increased training time and trainable parameters compared to our custom ConvNet.

Additionally, Table~\ref{tab:conv_vs_res_emb} compares two coreset selection setups. 
In the first case, we train our latent ConvNet embedder and use its feature space on which cosine $k$-center selection is performed. 
Each subset (from 10\% to 90\% of the training data) is then used to train a classifier with the same ConvNet. 
In the second case, we repeat the entire procedure using a ResNet-18 as latent embedder instead.

Across all data coresets, the ConvNet selected coreset consistently yields substantially higher test accuracy than the ResNet-18-based one (e.g., 53.09\% vs.\ 39.46\% at 10\%, and 76.32\% vs.\ 44.20\% at 90\%). 
This suggests that, under a fixed training coreset, a deeper embedder does not automatically provide a better feature space for coreset selection.
Thus, ResNet-18 appears undertrained in this setting and produces less useful embeddings for making coreset selection less efficient. 
In practice, a compact latent ConvNet can be a more effective and computationally efficient choice for coreset selection.
